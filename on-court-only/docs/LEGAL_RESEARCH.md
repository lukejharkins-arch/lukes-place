## 1. Trade marks (Trade Marks Act 1995)

### 1.1 Who owns the marks we will be dealing with
NBA team names and logos, the NBA logoman and team marks are typically registered in Australia by NBA Properties, Inc. Manufacturer marks belong to Nike, Jordan Brand, adidas, Champion, Reebok and others. University names and logos belong to each institution, often managed through a licensing agent. **[UNVERIFIED for each specific mark.]** Check each mark on Australian Trade Mark Search before you use it:
https://search.ipaustralia.gov.au/trademarks/search/quick

### 1.2 Section 120: infringement
A person infringes a registered mark if they use, *as a trade mark*, a sign that is substantially identical or deceptively similar to it:
- **s120(1):** in relation to the goods or services it is registered for;
- **s120(2):** in relation to goods or services of the same description, or closely related ones, unless there is no likelihood of deception or confusion;
- **s120(3):** in relation to unrelated goods or services, if the mark is well known in Australia and the use would indicate a connection with the owner that harms their interests.

The threshold question is always whether the sign is being used **"as a trade mark"**, meaning as a badge of origin for *our* goods or services.

Recent authority shows the courts look hard at what counts as "use":
- In *Fanatics, LLC v FanFirm Pty Ltd* [2025] FCAFC 87, the Full Federal Court held that a mark appearing on information labels was **not** trade mark use in the course of trade.
- The court also stressed that using a mark *on goods* is distinct from using it *in relation to retail services*.
- The court dismissed Fanatics' appeal. Commentary reports that the High Court refused special leave.
- Sources: Spruson & Ferguson, https://www.spruson.com/australia-fanatics-trademark-case/ ; Madderns, https://madderns.com.au/use-on-goods-is-not-use-in-retail-fanfirm-v-fanatics-and-a-cautionary-tale-in-trade-mark-use/ ; Lavan, https://www.lavan.com.au/publications/fanatics-appeal-considers-ecommerce-trade-mark-use/

This case also matters for our own brand. A sports-merchandise retailer with a large US brand lost to a smaller Australian business that had **earlier continuous use** in Australia. Clear "ON COURT ONLY" early and file early (see section 10).

### 1.3 Section 122: defences, including descriptive use
The s122 defences relevant to us:
- **s122(1)(b)(i):** using a sign *in good faith* to indicate the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin or some other characteristic of goods or services.
- **s122(1)(c):** using the trade mark *in good faith* to indicate the intended purpose of goods (accessories, parts and the like).
- **s122(1)(e):** exercising a right to use a mark that the person holds under the Act.

Good faith means honesty. The party relying on the defence must prove it. A defence will fail where the user is trying to trade on someone else's reputation.
Source: Duncan Cotterill, https://duncancotterill.com/insights/understanding-good-faith-in-australian-trade-mark-defences/ ; LegalVision, https://legalvision.com.au/defences-trade-mark-infringement

In *Fanatics*, the good-faith defences (s122(1)(a) own-name and s122(1)(b)) and honest concurrent use (s44(3)) failed because Fanatics knew of FanFirm's prior use.
Source: Piper Alderman, https://piperalderman.com.au/insight/fanfirm-pty-ltd-v-fanatics-llc-defences-under-the-trade-marks-act/

### 1.4 Section 122A: the parallel importation defence
**What it is:**
- s122A replaced former s123.
- It was introduced by the *Intellectual Property Laws Amendment (Productivity Commission Response Part 1 and Other Measures) Act 2018*.
- It commenced on **25 August 2018** and applies to infringement proceedings started on or after that date.
- The brief refers to "2019 amendments". The parallel-import change is the 2018 Act. The 24 February 2019 date belongs to a different change, the non-use grace period.
- Sources: Clayton Utz, https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2018/october/shades-of-grey-new-parallel-importation-defence-to-trade-mark-infringement-in-australia ; Lawpath, https://lawpath.com.au/blog/key-changes-trade-marks-act-1995-cth ; K&L Gates, https://www.klgates.com/Change-is-Coming-to-Australian-Parallel-Importation-law--What-do-you-Need-to-Know-01-25-20181

**What it requires (summary):** There is no infringement where:
1. the goods are "similar" to the registered goods; and
2. before using the mark, the person made **reasonable inquiries** about it; and
3. a reasonable person, having made those inquiries, would have concluded at the time of use that the mark **had been applied to the goods by, or with the consent of**:
   - the registered owner;
   - an authorised user;
   - a person permitted to use it by the owner or an authorised user;
   - a person with significant influence over the owner's use; or
   - an associated entity of any of these.

**Why the 2018 change matters:**
- The old s123 needed consent from the *Australian* registered owner. In *Paul's Retail Pty Ltd v Lonsdale Australia Ltd* [2012] FCAFC 130, the importer failed because the Australian owner had not consented, even though the European goods were made under licence.
- s122A was designed to close that gap. Licence conditions (for example "not for sale in Australia") and assignment structures no longer defeat the defence where the mark was applied with the consent of someone in the brand-owner's group.
- Sources: Clayton Utz (above) ; World Trademark Review on Lonsdale, https://www.worldtrademarkreview.com/article/parallel-importers-fail-again-full-court-of-federal-court ; Mondaq, https://www.mondaq.com/australia/trademark/208034/nowhere-to-run-for-grey-goods-importers-full-federal-court-restricts-parallel-importation-defence

**How it applies to ON COURT ONLY:**

| Scenario | Likely s122A position | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Genuine Nike/adidas/Champion NBA jersey, warm-up or shooting shirt made under NBA licence and issued to a team, bought from the team, a team-authorised sale, a reputable dealer or a collector | **Defence likely available.** The marks were applied by or with consent of the brand-owner group. | Keep evidence of "reasonable inquiries": provenance, tags, supplier invoices, team letters of authenticity, photo matching. |
| Genuine NCAA team-issued gear (school marks applied by the licensed outfitter) | **Likely available**, on the same reasoning | Same evidence requirements |
| Counterfeit or "replica" goods sold as genuine | **No defence.** Infringement and ACL breach. | Highest risk in this category. Authentication is the legal defence, not just a marketing feature. |
| Genuine blank garment with team marks or lettering **added later by an unauthorised third party** (aftermarket "pro-cut" customisation) | **Defence doubtful** for the added marks, which were not applied with consent | Treat as non-genuine unless the team or authorised supplier applied the marks |
| Genuine item that was **stolen** or diverted (for example from an equipment room) | s122A may still cover the *mark*, because it was applied with consent, but there is a **title** problem: no good title passes, and ACL s51 guarantees title | Provenance checks protect against both issues. **[LAWYER]** |
| Genuine goods re-branded or altered (for example removing tags, "restoring" logos) | Risk that it is no longer the owner's goods in the same condition | Do not alter branded items. Disclose repairs. **[LAWYER]** |

**Bottom line (research view):** Importing and reselling **genuine** NBA/NCAA team-issued apparel is, in principle, lawful in Australia under s122A. This holds where (a) the marks were applied by or with the consent of the brand-owner group, and (b) we can show we made reasonable inquiries before selling.

The defence is untested at appellate level for this kind of collectible trade. Its success depends entirely on evidence of genuineness. This is the core question for a lawyer. **[LAWYER]**

**Practical risk:** brand owners can lodge a **Notice of Objection** with the ABF (Trade Marks Act Part 13), and the ABF can then seize imports it suspects are counterfeit. Major sports brands commonly use this scheme. If a shipment is seized we would need to claim its release and may have to prove genuineness. Budget time and money for this, and keep purchase documents with each shipment. **[UNVERIFIED for whether NBA Properties, Nike or particular universities have notices lodged. Ask the ABF or a customs broker.]**

### 1.5 Describing genuine goods vs using logos in our own branding
**Generally acceptable** (descriptive or nominative use, plus s122A for the goods themselves):
- Product titles and descriptions that truthfully name the team, league, manufacturer, player and season, for example: "Team-issued Chicago Bulls Nike warm-up pants, 2003-04, size 3XL+2".
- Photos of the actual item showing its own logos.
- Factual filters and categories ("Shop by team: Lakers") that use word marks to describe stock, provided they are not styled as logos.

**High risk. Avoid unless licensed:**
- Using team, NBA or NCAA **logos** (the logoman, team crests, university seals or marks) in our own logo, site header, packaging, swing tags, social avatars, ad creative or merchandise.
- Using marks as **keywords in a way that suggests affiliation**, for example "Official NBA store" or "NBA Authentic Australia".
- Domain names, social handles or business names containing team or league marks.
- Bidding on trade-marked terms in search ads where the ad copy suggests affiliation. The bidding itself is usually lower risk than the ad copy. **[LAWYER]**

**Required disclaimer (site footer and About page):**
> "ON COURT ONLY is an independent reseller of pre-owned and surplus apparel. We are not affiliated with, endorsed by or sponsored by the NBA, any NBA team, the NCAA, any college or university, or any manufacturer. All trade marks belong to their owners and are used only to describe the genuine goods we sell."

A disclaimer helps, but it does **not** cure a misleading overall impression. The ACL looks at the dominant message.

## 2. Copyright (Copyright Act 1968)

### 2.1 The issue
- Photographs are artistic works. The photographer, or their agency or employer, usually owns copyright. NBA game photography is largely owned or controlled by NBA Entertainment and distributed via Getty Images. College photos are owned by the school, conference or wire agencies (AP, Getty, USA Today).
- Reproducing or communicating a photo online without a licence infringes copyright (ss31 and 36 for artistic works). **[UNVERIFIED section references. Check the current compilation.]**
- The fair dealing exceptions are narrow and purpose-specific: research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, and reporting news (ss40–42). They will **not** cover using a game photo to sell a product.

### 2.2 Getty Images (and similar agencies)
- **Editorial vs commercial:** Getty licences prohibit use of **editorial** content for "commercial, promotional, advertorial, endorsement, advertising, gambling or merchandising purposes" without express written consent and an additional fee.
  - Editorial images have no model or property releases.
  - Product pages and ads selling memorabilia are commercial or promotional use.
  - Sources: Getty Images Content Licence Agreement, https://www.gettyimages.in/license-info ; Getty Help Centre, https://www.gettyimages.ca/faq/working-files
- **Getty embeds:** Getty's free embed tool also excludes commercial use, "for example, in advertising, promotions or merchandising, or to suggest endorsement or sponsorship".
  - Source: Computerworld on Getty embed terms, https://www.computerworld.com/article/1383852/getty-makes-35-million-images-embeddable-to-gather-data-monetize-usage.html ; Creative Commons blog, https://creativecommons.org/2014/03/07/getty-images-allows-free-embed-of-35-million-photographs/
- **Practical conclusion:** Do not use Getty, AP or team photos on product pages or in ads, even with an editorial licence or embed.
  - A commercial licence for an image showing an identifiable NBA player in NBA uniform will usually also need NBA, team and player clearances. These are unlikely to be granted to an unaffiliated reseller.
  - If you want "photo-matching" evidence (a game photo showing the item worn), keep it as **internal authentication evidence**. Do not publish it unless it is properly licensed.

### 2.3 Wikimedia Commons
- Many Commons files are under CC BY, CC BY-SA or public domain. These licences generally **allow commercial use with attribution**, and share-alike for BY-SA.
- Three caveats:
  1. Check each file's licence individually. Some uploads are wrongly licensed.
  2. Commons licences cover **copyright only**. They do not clear trade marks, personality or endorsement issues, which Commons itself flags with its "non-copyright restrictions" notice.
  3. CC BY-SA can require derivative images to be shared under the same licence.
- **[UNVERIFIED this session. Check https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Reusing_content_outside_Wikimedia]**
- Even with a valid licence, using a player's photo **next to a product** may imply endorsement. See ACL (section 3) and personality rights (section 5).

### 2.4 Linking rather than embedding
- A plain hyperlink to a page where a photo is lawfully published (for example, a team's game recap) is generally lower risk than reproducing the image. Australian law on whether linking or embedding amounts to "communication" is not settled. **[LAWYER]**
- Framing or embedding that makes the image appear part of our page carries more risk, and Getty's terms expressly restrict it.
- **Recommended:** Use **our own photography** of each item. We own that copyright. Showing the item's own logos is acceptable (section 1.5). Link out to official sources for provenance context, and keep licensed editorial imagery for genuinely editorial content (a blog or "story" pages) only if properly licensed.
- **Consignor photos:** Our consignment terms must give us a licence to use any photos the consignor supplies, and must warrant that the consignor owns them.

## 3. Australian Consumer Law (ACL, Competition and Consumer Act 2010 Sch 2)

### 3.1 Section 18: misleading or deceptive conduct
- A person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive.
- Intention is irrelevant. The test is the overall impression on the ordinary, reasonable member of the target audience, here collectors and fans.
- Silence, half-truths and fine print can mislead.

### 3.2 Section 29: false or misleading representations (civil penalties)
**[Paragraph letters taken from background knowledge; one secondary source conflicted. Verify the current text.]** Relevant paragraphs:
- **s29(1)(a):** goods are of a particular standard, quality, value, grade, composition, style or model, **or have had a particular history or particular previous use**. This is the direct hook for "team-issued", "player-issued", "game-worn", "photo-matched" and "worn by [player]" claims.
- **s29(1)(c):** goods are **new**. We must not describe used goods as new. "New with tags" means exactly that.
- **s29(1)(e)–(f):** false or misleading testimonials.
- **s29(1)(g):** goods have **sponsorship, approval**, performance characteristics, uses or benefits they do not have. "Officially licensed by the NBA" is a risk; "NBA-approved" is wrong.
- **s29(1)(h):** the person making the representation (us) has a **sponsorship, approval or affiliation** it does not have.
- **s29(1)(i):** false or misleading representations about price.
- **s29(1)(k):** place of origin.
- **s29(1)(m):** consumer guarantee rights. "No refunds" statements are misleading.

Source on s29(1)(a) and (h): https://australiancontractlaw.info/legislation/acl/s29

**Penalties [UNVERIFIED this session]:** Since November 2022 the maximum corporate penalty for s29 is the greatest of A$50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover during the breach period. Individuals face up to A$2.5 million. s18 carries no pecuniary penalty but supports damages, injunctions and corrective orders.

### 3.3 Implications for "team-issued" and "game-worn" claims
Each provenance claim is a representation about **history and previous use** (s29(1)(a)). It also forms part of the goods' **description**, so the consumer guarantee of correspondence with description (s56) applies. Recommendations:

1. **Adopt a published, defined taxonomy.** Example tiers:
   - "Retail" (never called team-issued)
   - "Team-issued" (made for and supplied to the team; not necessarily worn)
   - "Player-issued" (issued to a named player)
   - "Player-worn"
   - "Game-worn" (worn in an identified game)
   - "Photo-matched" (matched to a dated image)

   Publish exact definitions and evidence standards. This matches the founder direction in `reference/codex_pack/README_START_HERE.md`.
2. **Put the evidence on each listing:** source type (team sale, auction house, collector), letters of authenticity, tag details, and what has *not* been verified.
3. **Hedge honestly where appropriate**, for example "attributed to", "consistent with team-issue specifications", "not photo-matched". Hedges must reflect the real uncertainty; they cannot contradict the headline.
4. **Previous owner claims:** "From the collection of…" or "ex-[team] equipment room" claims need documentary support.
5. **"Verified" or "Authenticated" badges are our own representations.** If a marketplace listing carries our badge and the item is fake, we are exposed under s18/s29 even though a third-party seller made the item claim. Define the badge's meaning precisely and keep audit records.
6. **Condition grading:** disclose stains, alterations, re-lettering, missing tags and repairs.
7. **Price comparisons** ("valued at", "was") must be genuine and substantiated.

### 3.4 Consumer guarantees and second-hand goods
- The guarantees apply to goods sold to consumers in trade or commerce, **whether new or second-hand**. The goods must be of acceptable quality "having regard to" price, age and condition, so second-hand goods are judged against reasonable expectations for used goods.
- Defects drawn to the buyer's attention before sale, or that an examination should have revealed, are not a failure.
- Source: ACCC, Consumer rights and guarantees, https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees ; ACCC Consumer guarantees guide for businesses, https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/Consumer%20guarantees%20-%20A%20guide%20for%20businesses%20and%20legal%20practitioners.pdf ; ACCC durability guidance, https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/acl-consumer-guarantee-guidance-durability.pdf

Key guarantees for us:
- **s51 title:** clear title. This is relevant to stolen or diverted gear.
- **s52 undisturbed possession.**
- **s53 no undisclosed securities:** relevant to consigned goods subject to someone's security interest.
- **s54 acceptable quality**, adjusted for age and condition.
- **s56 correspond with description:** the team-issued or game-worn claim.
- **s57 match a sample or demonstration model:** listing photos.

Other points:
- Guarantees **cannot be excluded**. "All sales final" is misleading if it implies otherwise. You may have a "no change-of-mind returns" policy, while still honouring guarantees.
- **Private sales:** sales by private individuals not in business are outside the guarantees. In a marketplace phase, collector-to-collector sales may fall outside, but **our** services (authentication, platform, payment handling) are within. Auction-sale carve-outs exist for some guarantees. **[LAWYER]**

### 3.5 Unfair contract terms
**[UNVERIFIED this session]** Since 9 November 2023, unfair terms in standard-form consumer and small-business contracts are prohibited and attract penalties. This covers consignment agreements, seller terms and buyer terms. Avoid one-sided terms such as:
- unilateral fee changes without notice;
- broad liability exclusions;
- indefinite holding of consignor stock;
- the right to sell below the agreed reserve.

## 4. Passing off

The common-law tort protects goodwill. It needs three elements:
1. reputation or goodwill in Australia;
2. a misrepresentation, for example that we are connected with the NBA, a team, a school or a player, or that our goods are theirs when they are not;
3. resulting damage or likely damage.

It runs in parallel with ACL ss18 and 29, and claims are usually pleaded together.

Risk areas for us:
- a brand get-up (colours, fonts, iconography) that echoes NBA or team identity;
- words like "Official", "Authorised" or "Pro Shop";
- player imagery implying endorsement.

Selling genuine goods under accurate descriptions is not passing off. Our brand must stand clearly as **independent**. Note that the *Fanatics* Full Court rejected the passing off and ACL claims on its facts, which shows these claims depend heavily on the evidence. **[LAWYER]**

## 5. Personality and likeness rights

### 5.1 Australia
- **No statutory or general right of publicity.** Protection comes through passing off, ACL ss18 and 29, and sometimes defamation.
  - Source: Arts Law Centre, https://www.artslaw.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Unauthorised_Use_of_Your_Image_2016.pdf ; McCullough Robertson Luxury Law Guide 2024, https://mccullough.com.au/2024/06/11/luxury-law-guide-australia-2024-part-2/ ; Mills Oakley/MK on Dua Lipa v Samsung, https://mk.com.au/dua-lipa-v-samsung-lessons-for-australian-businesses-on-celebrity-image-rights/
- **Henderson v Radio Corporation Pty Ltd (1960) 60 SR (NSW) 576:** professional dancers succeeded in passing off over their image on a record cover. It is the foundation of "false endorsement" passing off.
- **Pacific Dunlop v Hogan (1989)** and **Talmax Pty Ltd v Telstra Corp Ltd [1997] 2 Qd R 444** (Olympic swimmer Kieren Perkins) are further sports and celebrity examples. **[UNVERIFIED this session. Read the judgments before citing.]**
- **Practical rule:**
  - Naming the player whose item it is, as an accurate description, is generally acceptable.
  - Using the player's **face or image** in ads, hero banners or brand content suggests endorsement and is high risk.

### 5.2 USA: right of publicity
- This is state law and varies by state. Examples include California Civil Code §3344 plus common law, New York Civil Rights Law §§50–51, and Illinois' Right of Publicity Act. **[UNVERIFIED this session.]**
- Selling a genuine item while truthfully identifying who wore it is generally treated differently from using a name or likeness to **advertise** unrelated goods. Commercial advertising using images remains the risk.
- Where we target US customers, or approach US teams and players, take US counsel's view on our ad creative. **[LAWYER (US)]**

### 5.3 NCAA NIL context
- Since mid-2021, college athletes can be paid for their name, image and likeness (NIL).
- The *House v NCAA* settlement led to direct revenue-sharing by schools, NIL deal oversight by a new **College Sports Commission**, and NCAA Division I bylaw changes adopted in October 2025. Sources:
  - Mondaq, https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/sport/1636670/house-v-ncaa-settlement-approved-changing-the-landscape-of-college-sports
  - CRS, https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11349.html
  - Front Office Sports, https://frontofficesports.com/theres-a-new-nil-enforcement-body-in-college-sports-its-not-the-ncaa/
  - NCAA Q&A (updated 29 May 2026), https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/governance/d1/legislation/May2026D1Gov_PhaseEightSetQuestionandAnswer.pdf
- **For us:** team-issued gear is usually **institutional property**, not the athlete's.
  - Buy from the institution, through official surplus or equipment sales or school-run auctions, or with written institutional authority. Do not buy directly from current student-athletes. That creates property-ownership and eligibility or rules risks for the athlete, and reputational risk for us.
  - Any marketing that features an athlete's NIL (for example "Shop [Player]'s worn gear" with their image) is NIL use that would normally need the athlete's agreement, and possibly the school's.

## 6. Imports (customs, GST at the border, biosecurity)

### 6.1 Import declaration threshold and GST/duty at the border
- **Goods valued at A$1,000 or less** arriving by air or sea cargo are generally cleared with a Self-Assessed Clearance (SAC) declaration. They are **generally free of duty and GST at the border**; tobacco and alcohol are excluded from this concession.
- **Goods valued over A$1,000** need a full **Import Declaration**, and duty, GST and the import processing charge are payable.
- **International mail:** SAC declarations are not used for postal articles. Under-threshold postal consignments need no declaration.
- **Aggregation:** packages forming one order are one consignment. You cannot split an order to stay under A$1,000.
- Sources: ABF, Import declarations, https://www.abf.gov.au/imports/Pages/How-to-import/Import-declarations.aspx ; ABF Self-Assessed Clearance fact sheet, https://www.abf.gov.au/trade-and-goods-compliance-subsite/files/fact-sheets/self-assessed-clearances-declarations-fs.pdf ; Australian Customs Notice 2021/01, https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/CustomsNotices/2021-01.pdf

### 6.2 GST on low-value imported goods (point-of-sale collection)
- Since 1 July 2018, overseas vendors, electronic distribution platforms (eBay, for example) and redeliverers must collect 10% GST at the point of sale on sales of low-value goods (customs value A$1,000 or less) to Australian **consumers**.
  - Source: Department of Home Affairs Notice 2018/13, https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/customsnotices/2018-13.pdf ; ATO, GST and Australian businesses importing goods, https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/gst-excise-and-indirect-taxes/gst/in-detail/rules-for-specific-transactions/international-transactions/australian-business-importing-goods-and-services
- **For us:** once GST-registered, give overseas sellers and platforms our **ABN and GST-registered status** so that the sale is treated as business-to-business and GST is not charged. Where GST is charged in error, the ATO page explains the position.
- Before registration, the 10% GST charged by eBay US and similar platforms is a real cost. **[ACCOUNTANT]**
- Above A$1,000, GST is collected **at the border** on the value of the taxable importation. That is customs value plus duty plus international transport and insurance. GST-registered importers can usually claim it back as an input tax credit, or defer it under the Deferred GST scheme. **[ACCOUNTANT/BROKER]**

### 6.3 Customs duty on apparel: were clothing tariffs removed?

Short answer: **partly, and the position changed in July 2026. Confirm each line with a broker.**

1. **Rates still showing on the ABF tariff in search extracts.** The ABF current tariff, Schedule 3, Chapter 61 (knitted apparel), showed a **5% general rate** in search extracts for:
   - 6114.30.00: garments of man-made fibres, which can cover many polyester jerseys and warm-ups;
   - 6110.20.00: cotton jerseys and pullovers;
   - 6110.11.00: wool.

   Source: https://www.abf.gov.au/importing-exporting-and-manufacturing/tariff-classification/current-tariff/schedule-3/section-xi/chapter-61 . The extract date is unknown, and full page fetch was blocked.
2. **First nuisance-tariff tranche, 1 July 2024.** About 457 "nuisance" tariffs were abolished from that date, including some clothing lines (about 12 clothing items and 3 footwear lines, per industry coverage).
   - Source: Treasury consultation, https://treasury.gov.au/consultation/c2024-506306 ; Explanatory Statement F2024L00659, https://legislation.gov.au/F2024L00659/asmade/2024-06-07/es/original/pdf ; Ragtrader, https://www.ragtrader.com.au/news/industry-reacts-as-nuisance-tariffs-hit-the-government-chopping-block
3. **Second tranche, 1 July 2026.** A further **497** nuisance tariffs were removed from that date. This was announced 12 May 2026, given effect by Customs Tariff Proposal (No. 1) 2026, and followed by a bill introduced 24 June 2026.
   - The government said it covers goods "including certain household and consumer wares, personal care products, **clothing and textiles**". The rationale is that few importers pay the 5% general rate because of FTAs and concessions.
   - Source: ABF Australian Customs Notice 2026/20, https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/CustomsNotices/2026-20.pdf ; ACN 2026/21, https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/CustomsNotices/2026-21.pdf ; Baker McKenzie, https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/05/australia-budget-bites-nuisance-tariffs-and-trusted-trader-program ; House debate 24 June 2026, https://www.openaustralia.org.au/debates/?id=2026-06-24.6.1&m=681
   - **Not verified:** which specific Chapter 61/62 lines (jerseys, warm-ups, shorts) were included. ACN 2026/20 has the list but could not be read in this session. **[BROKER]**
4. **Duty depends on country of ORIGIN, not the country we ship from.** Most NBA and NCAA apparel is manufactured in Asia (for example Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, China), not the USA.
   - **US-origin goods:** can enter free under the Australia–US FTA, which uses a strict "yarn-forward" rule for textiles. Source: DFAT AUSFTA guide, textiles and apparel, https://www.dfat.gov.au/about-us/publications/trade-investment/australia-united-states-free-trade-agreement-guide-to-the-agreement/Pages/4-textiles-and-apparel
   - **Asian-origin goods:** may qualify for a Free rate under ChAFTA, AANZFTA, RCEP, CPTPP or a bilateral FTA, but only with origin evidence. Second-hand resellers usually **cannot obtain** origin documents.
   - **Without origin evidence:** the **general rate** applies. That is 5% on lines still dutiable, or Free if the line was removed in 2024 or 2026.
5. **Practical position:**
   - For **shipments of A$1,000 or less**, duty and GST at the border are generally **nil**. Most of our early imports (around 30 items over several shipments) are likely to fall under this, as long as each order is genuinely separate.
   - For **larger consolidated shipments**, assume **0–5% duty** depending on the tariff line, **plus 10% GST** and charges, until a broker confirms the lines.

The commercial BICON case for "used clothing" was **not** retrievable this session.

## 8. Consignment

### 8.1 Legal nature
- A consignment where the consignor keeps ownership until sale is typically a **bailment for sale**, coupled with an **agency** to sell. We hold the goods as bailee and must take reasonable care.
- On sale, we account to the consignor for the price less commission.
- Our agreement should cover:
  - ownership and risk;
  - insurance (our policy must cover goods held for others);
  - reserve or minimum price and our pricing authority;
  - commission and fees;
  - payment timing, for example X days after the return window closes;
  - returns and chargebacks;
  - authentication outcomes, including what happens if an item fails authentication: return at consignor's cost; never sell;
  - storage period and abandonment;
  - photo licence;
  - consignor warranties: title, genuineness, no security interests, lawful export;
  - indemnity;
  - privacy;
  - termination.

### 8.2 PPSA considerations
- Under the PPSA, a **"commercial consignment"** is deemed to be a security interest, whether or not it secures payment.
  - It applies where the consignor keeps an interest in goods delivered to a consignee, **and both the consignor and consignee deal in goods of that kind in the ordinary course of business**.
  - Exceptions apply, including where the consignee is generally known to its creditors to sell goods for others, and consumer-property cases.
  - Sources: PPSR, Commercial consignment, https://www.ppsr.gov.au/commercial-consignment ; Bright Law, https://www.brightlaw.com.au/consignments-and-personal-property-securities/ ; AGD explanatory memorandum on reforms, https://consultations.ag.gov.au/legal-system/government-response-to-pps-review/user_uploads/explanatory-memorandum-sch-1-ch-4-pps-framework-reforms.pdf
- **What this means for us:**
  - **Private collectors** consigning personal collections are generally **not** "commercial consignors", so the PPSA deeming does not apply. Ordinary bailment and property law govern.
  - **Dealers and business consignors** (other resellers, team equipment managers acting commercially, auction businesses) may be commercial consignors.
    - If they **do not register** on the PPSR, their goods could become available to our secured creditors, or to a liquidator if we became insolvent. This is the PPSA "vesting" rule. **[UNVERIFIED section number; s267 is believed to be the vesting provision]**
    - Sophisticated consignors will want to **register**, with a purchase-money security interest (PMSI) registered within the statutory time limits.
  - **We do not register** anything as consignee. If we take a loan secured by a **general security agreement**, consigned stock must be carved out or clearly identified.
  - **Good practice:** segregate consigned stock in the inventory system. Make our consignment business publicly known, for example in website and terms, which also supports the "generally known" exception. Tell business consignors they may register. Before buying any high-value item outright, search the PPSR for existing security interests over it.
- The PPS framework reforms were proposed; whether they have passed is **[UNVERIFIED]**. **[LAWYER]**

## 9. Marketplace, privacy and electronic marketing

### 9.1 Marketplace operator obligations (Phase B: verified collector marketplace)
- The ACL applies to platforms acting in trade or commerce. A marketplace can be liable under s18 for its own conduct, and potentially for listings it adopts or endorses. **"Verified" badges and authentication claims are especially risky because they are our own representations.**
  - Source: LegalVision, https://legalvision.com.au/acl-online-marketplace
- **ACCC priorities:** platform liability, misleading pricing and "dark patterns" in online design. Treasury is consulting on **mandatory product-safety obligations for online marketplaces**, and the voluntary Product Safety Pledge exists. A Competition and Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Bill 2026 is referenced in commentary, but its status is **[UNVERIFIED]**.
  - Sources: Best Lawyers on ACCC 2026–29 strategy, https://www.bestlawyers.com/article/unpacking-the-accc-s-2026-29-strategy-and-the/7604 ; King & Wood Mallesons, https://www.mallesons.com/au/en/insights/latest-thinking/australias-product-safety-laws-play-catch-up-with-online-marketplaces.html ; ACCC Product Safety Pledge release, https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/online-marketplaces-pledge-to-do-more-to-protect-consumers-from-unsafe-products-0
- **Marketplace design implications:**
  - seller identity verification (know your seller);
  - clear seller-of-record disclosure on each listing ("Sold by [collector], authenticated by ON COURT ONLY to [standard]");
  - a takedown process for IP complaints from brand owners;
  - a counterfeit and stolen-goods policy;
  - a dispute and refund process;
  - transparent fees;
  - no fake urgency or dark patterns;
  - drip-pricing compliance (show the total price including mandatory fees).
- **Payments:** handling buyer funds for third parties should go through a regulated payments provider such as Stripe Connect or Shopify Payments, not our own trust account. This avoids financial services licensing questions. **[LAWYER]**

### 9.2 Privacy Act 1988
- **Small business exemption:** businesses with annual turnover of **A$3 million or less** are generally exempt. The exemption does **not** apply if, among other things, the business trades in personal information (discloses it for a benefit, or collects it from others for a benefit).
- **Reform status:** the Government agreed "in principle" to remove the exemption. As at the sources reviewed, the general exemption had **not** been removed. A partial withdrawal applies from **1 July 2026** to AML/CTF Tranche 2 reporting entities; that should not cover a clothing reseller.
  - Sources: HWL Ebsworth, https://hwlebsworth.com.au/small-businesses-big-change-privacy-obligations-under-tranche-2-of-the-aml-ctf-reforms/ ; MinterEllison, https://www.minterellison.com/articles/aml-ctf-reforms-dont-forget-the-privacy-policy ; Mondaq, https://www.mondaq.com/australia/privacy-protection/1409910/new-privacy-laws-incoming-australian-government-releases-response-to-the-privacy-act-review-report
  - Check the OAIC for the current position: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-legislation/the-privacy-act
- **[UNVERIFIED this session]** A statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy took effect in 2025 and is not limited by business size.
- **Good practice regardless:**
  - publish a privacy policy (Shopify, Stripe and Google also require one);
  - collect the minimum consignor ID needed for provenance and anti-fraud;
  - store ID documents securely and delete them on a schedule;
  - get consent for marketing;
  - adopt a data breach response plan.

### 9.3 Spam Act 2003 (Australia)
Applies to **commercial electronic messages** (email, SMS, instant messages, and probably social or LinkedIn direct messages) with an "Australian link". **Sending from Australia is enough**, so the Act covers our outreach to **US teams and universities** too. Three rules:
1. **Consent:**
   - Express consent: keep records of who, when and how.
   - Or inferred consent: from an existing relationship, or from **conspicuous publication** of a work address where the message relates directly to the recipient's role and there is no "no marketing" statement next to it.
   - Inferred consent is narrow. A US equipment manager's published email may support a relevant B2B enquiry about surplus team gear. A generic "info@" address with a "no solicitations" note does not.
2. **Identify:** accurate sender name, business name and contact details.
3. **Unsubscribe:** a functional unsubscribe in every message, honoured within **5 business days**.

Purely factual messages have limited exceptions.
Sources: ACMA, Avoid sending spam, https://www.acma.gov.au/theACMA/spam-industry-obligations ; Spam Act 2003 (legislation.gov.au, C2004A01214) ; Pointon Partners, https://pointonpartners.com.au/spam-act/

### 9.4 US CAN-SPAM Act (15 U.S.C. §7701 et seq.) **[UNVERIFIED this session; based on FTC guidance from background knowledge]**
- Opt-out model: no prior consent needed for commercial email.
- Requirements:
  - accurate header and "from" information;
  - non-deceptive subject lines;
  - identify the message as an advertisement where it is one;
  - include a valid **physical postal address**;
  - provide a clear opt-out and honour it within **10 business days**.
- Check the FTC guide: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business
- **Comply with both regimes.** In practice, the Australian Spam Act is stricter on consent and governs us as sender, while CAN-SPAM adds the physical address requirement.
- University and team addresses may also be subject to institutional policies. Some state laws (for example on SMS) are stricter. **Do not buy email lists.**
